---
title: I'm truly sorry!
date: 2026-11-19
excerpt: To my wife and kids, I am truly sorry.
---

To my wife and kids, I am truly sorry.

To the hundreds of kids I coached in football, basketball, baseball, and soccer, I am truly sorry.

To almost all the youth coaches of every sport out there, it’s time to admit we were wrong.

To most parents whose kids played competitive sports, it’s time to admit we were wrong.

To a significant number of spouses out there, it’s time to admit we were wrong.

And to the teachers, bosses, and just about everyone else who has occupied a position of authority over the last several decades, perhaps it’s time for a little self-reflection.

To the thousands of psychologists, psychiatrists, therapists, and pharmaceutical companies, you should probably send us all thank-you notes.

We helped create one hell of a customer base.

So what the fuck am I talking about?!

ANXIETY.

The relentless, exhausting, sometimes debilitating anxiety that seems to have invaded almost every corner of modern life.

As most of you know, I have a PhD in absolutely nothing. Unless, of course, someone has started awarding doctorates in common sense, sarcasm, and the ability to piss people off without really trying.

But something I read this morning hit me like a two-by-four.

And it made me think about something that has been bothering me for years.

All around me, I see people suffering from anxiety. Some mildly. Others severely. Some to the point where their lives are almost completely controlled by it.

These are smart, successful, wonderful people.

People who seemingly have everything.

And yet, for some of them, getting through an ordinary Tuesday can feel like preparing for a hostage negotiation.

I’ve spent a considerable amount of time reading about anxiety, trying to understand where it comes from.

And I keep coming back to one enormous possibility.

What if our obsession with success, achievement, and perfection has helped create generations of people who are terrified of failure?

A friend once jokingly told me that anxiety was a “white people problem.”

We both laughed.

Of course, anxiety affects people of every race, background, and economic circumstance. But his comment got me thinking about the environment in which so many of my friends and I grew up.

We were pushed to succeed.

In school. In sports. In business. In life.

Winning mattered.

Grades mattered.

Getting into the right college mattered.

Being successful mattered.

And somewhere along the way, we started confusing achievement with happiness.

Then we had children.

And apparently decided that whatever pressure we experienced growing up was entirely insufficient.

We needed to turn the fucking dial to eleven.

When I was a kid, we rode bicycles, played neighborhood sports, got into trouble, and occasionally came home with injuries that today would require three specialists, a psychological evaluation, and a meeting with the school superintendent.

Our kids?

By age eight, they had Gold Delta Status.

Not because they were traveling to Paris to appreciate art.

Because little Johnny had a soccer tournament in fucking Des Moines.

And if Johnny didn’t make the elite travel team by age nine, apparently his chances of attending Harvard, becoming a billionaire, and finding true love were officially over.

We hired private coaches.

Then specialized coaches.

Then strength coaches.

Then mental-performance coaches.

We had children who needed a sports psychologist before they needed deodorant.

And I was part of it.

I coached hundreds of kids over the years. I loved every minute of it. I loved those kids. I wanted every one of them to succeed.

But looking back, I wonder how often my desire to make them better inadvertently taught them that being good wasn’t good enough.

That winning was expected.

That mistakes were something to avoid rather than something to learn from.

And that somewhere, somehow, someone was always keeping score.

Then there was school.

Holy fucking shit.

Four hours of homework after six or seven hours of school.

Advanced placement classes.

Tutors.

College counselors.

SAT prep.

ACT prep.

And the greatest academic breakthrough in modern history:

“Your child got a 32 on the ACT? Clearly, something is wrong.”

After a little testing, perhaps Johnny qualifies for extra time.

And behold!

A 36!

A medical miracle!

We have now accomplished two things.

First, Johnny has a documented condition that may require legitimate accommodations.

Second, we have somehow managed to turn those accommodations into another competitive advantage to brag about at cocktail parties.

I want to be clear: learning disabilities and other conditions are real, and appropriate accommodations are essential. My sarcasm is directed at the parents who turn every aspect of childhood into an arms race.

Because God forbid another family’s child gets into a better college.

We weren’t raising children anymore. We were managing little corporate acquisitions with orthodontic bills.

And then something truly bizarre happened.

The kids grew up.

They got into great colleges.

They got great jobs.

They made money.

They got married.

They had beautiful children.

They checked every fucking box we told them mattered.

And many of them were miserable.

Not all, of course.

But far too many.

Because we forgot to teach them something infinitely more important than how to win.

How to be okay when they lose.

We taught them to pursue perfection.

We didn’t teach them that perfection doesn’t exist.

We taught them to compete.

We didn’t teach them when to stop competing.

We taught them to work harder.

We didn’t teach them that sometimes doing absolutely nothing is a perfectly respectable accomplishment.

We taught them that success would make them happy.

And then acted surprised when success wasn’t enough.

Think about that for a minute.

We spent decades telling our children that happiness was waiting for them at the finish line.

The right school.

The right job.

The right salary.

The right house.

The right spouse.

The right fucking kitchen countertops.

And every time they crossed one finish line, we moved it.

Congratulations! You made partner!

Now become managing partner.

Congratulations! You bought a beautiful house!

Did you see the one the neighbors just bought?

Congratulations! Your kid made the varsity team!

Why isn’t he starting?

We turned life into a treadmill where the speed keeps increasing and nobody can find the goddamn off switch.

And then we wonder why everyone is anxious.

Of course, anxiety is far more complicated than parenting or perfectionism. Genetics, biology, trauma, health, and many other factors play significant roles. Anxiety disorders are real medical conditions, not simply the result of demanding parents.

But I believe our culture of relentless achievement has become a powerful contributor to the problem.

And now we’ve added social media.

Because apparently, comparing ourselves with our neighbors wasn’t psychologically destructive enough.

Now we can compare ourselves with eight billion people.

Your friend is in Italy.

Your neighbor bought a boat.

Your college roommate sold his company.

Some 27-year-old asshole on Instagram is explaining how he made $40 million working three hours a week from a beach in Bali.

Meanwhile, you’re sitting in your kitchen wondering why your life isn’t quite as spectacular.

Never mind that the guy in Bali may be renting the house, leasing the Lamborghini, and selling a $499 online course about financial freedom.

We have created an entire civilization of people competing against other people’s advertising campaigns.

And we’re losing.

Every single day.

Even worse, we have begun treating ordinary imperfections as emergencies.

A bad grade is a crisis.

A missed promotion is a crisis.

A breakup is a crisis.

A child who isn’t invited to a birthday party is a crisis.

Someone doesn’t respond to your text within seven minutes?

Clearly, the relationship is over.

Maybe they’re just taking a shit.

But no.

We need to analyze it.

Discuss it.

Process it.

And possibly schedule an appointment.

Somewhere along the way, we started losing our ability to tolerate ordinary discomfort.

And discomfort is part of life.

So is disappointment.

So is embarrassment.

So is failure.

So is being told no.

And here’s something that might come as a shock to the younger generations:

Sometimes you’re going to suck at something.

And that’s okay!

You don’t need a diagnosis because you can’t hit a curveball.

You don’t need a life coach because someone else got promoted.

And you certainly don’t need to discover your “authentic inner purpose” because your boss told you the PowerPoint presentation was shitty.

Sometimes the PowerPoint presentation was just shitty.

Fix it.

Laugh about it.

Move on.

But I also want to be careful here.

Someone experiencing severe anxiety can’t necessarily just laugh it off or decide to stop worrying. Professional treatment, including therapy and medication when appropriate, can be life-changing.

I’m not criticizing people who need help.

I’m questioning why we’ve built a culture that makes so many people feel they can never be enough.

And that brings me back to my apology.

To my wife.

To my children.

To the kids I coached.

To the people I have worked with.

I pushed.

I demanded.

I expected.

And while I genuinely believed I was helping people become the best versions of themselves, I wonder how often I forgot to tell them something far more important.

You are already enough.

Not because you won.

Not because you got an A.

Not because you made a million dollars.

Not because you got accepted into a prestigious university.

Not because you made your parents proud.

But because you’re a human being.

And your value isn’t determined by your accomplishments.

I still believe in ambition.

I still believe in working hard.

I still believe in excellence.

Hell, I still believe in winning.

I love winning.

But I have come to believe something much more important.

Winning at life and winning at everything are two entirely different things.

You can build a billion-dollar company and lose at life.

You can become the best athlete in the world and lose at life.

You can have a house full of awards, diplomas, and trophies and still wake up every morning feeling like you’re failing.

And you can have none of those things and live an extraordinarily happy, meaningful, beautiful life.

Maybe it’s time we started measuring success differently.

How often do you laugh?

How much time do you spend with people you love?

Do you have friends you can call when everything goes to shit?

Can you sit quietly without feeling guilty that you’re not being productive?

Can you enjoy someone else’s success without questioning your own?

Can you make a mistake without believing you’re a mistake?

Can you wake up in the morning and actually look forward to being alive?

Those seem like pretty fucking important measurements to me.

And perhaps the most important question of all:

Are the people around you happier because you’re in their lives?

I wish I had understood that question better 30 years ago.

I wish I had celebrated more mistakes.

I wish I had told more kids that losing a game didn’t matter.

I wish I had spent less time pushing and more time laughing.

And I wish I had understood that the greatest gift we can give our children isn’t an advantage over everyone else.

It’s the confidence to know they’ll be okay even when things don’t go their way.

I can’t go backward.

None of us can.

But we can change what we celebrate going forward.

We can stop asking children only what they accomplished today and start asking whether they had fun.

We can stop treating every setback as a catastrophe.

We can teach them that failure isn’t the opposite of success. It’s part of learning how to live.

And maybe, just maybe, we can start giving ourselves permission to be imperfect.

To be average at something.

To occasionally be terrible at something.

To have absolutely no fucking idea what we’re doing.

Because, believe me, most of us don’t.

Some people are simply much better at pretending.

And for those of you who are struggling with anxiety, I hope you find the support, understanding, and peace you deserve.

You don’t need another person telling you to try harder.

You’ve probably spent your entire life doing exactly that.

Maybe what you need is someone who tells you that you can stop trying to prove yourself for a little while.

Someone who loves you even when you fail.

Someone who reminds you that life isn’t supposed to be one continuous performance review.

And someone who makes you laugh when everything feels overwhelming.

I started this by apologizing to my wife, my kids, and hundreds of young athletes.

I meant it.

But I’m also incredibly grateful for everything they taught me.

And if I could coach those kids all over again, I’d still teach them to work hard, be disciplined, respect their teammates, and give everything they had.

I’d still want to win.

But after every game, win or lose, I’d make damn sure they knew that the scoreboard had absolutely nothing to do with how much they mattered.

And I’d probably take them all out for ice cream.

Even the kid who struck out four times.

Especially that kid.

Because 30 years from now, nobody is going to remember who won the fucking 10-year-old baseball championship.

But that kid might remember how you made him feel.

We spent generations teaching our children how to become successful.

Maybe it’s time we taught them how to become happy.

And while we’re at it, perhaps we adults should take the same class.

I suspect most of us would fail the first exam.

Which, for once, would be perfectly fucking okay.
